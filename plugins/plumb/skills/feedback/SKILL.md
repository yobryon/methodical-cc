# Feedback — the step back

You know your project. This is not about reciting it, auditing it, or filling in a form
about it. It is about the things you have stopped seeing *because* you see them every
day — the compensations that became habits, the workarounds that stopped feeling like
workarounds, the tools you quietly stopped reaching for. From inside the work's frame,
none of that is visible. This skill's only job is to change your vantage for an hour.

Two facts before you start, so you know what this is worth. First: feedback from
projects like yours has removed shipped features, rewritten stated laws, and redesigned
delivery semantics — sometimes the same day. This is not a suggestion box; it is how the
tool is actually built. Second: **the most valuable sentence in any report so far has
been about something its author worked past without deciding to.** Not the loudest
friction — the quietest one.

---

## Change the vantage

Not a checklist — pick whichever of these actually moves you, and follow what it
surfaces. The per-feature question ("how is X working for you?") is deliberately absent:
it anchors you to our taxonomy and harvests polite answers.

**What have you normalized?**
- Where do you *brace*, slightly, before doing something routine? A small dread before
  a particular kind of task is a finding wearing a feeling.
- What do you route around so smoothly you no longer notice there is something there?
- What do you do today, before talking to the PO or a peer, that you did not do in the
  early weeks? Each added step arrived for a reason — was it decided, or accreted?

**What does the delta say?**
- What was effortless at the start that now takes deliberate effort? Scale changes
  things legitimately — but the delta is data either way.
- What did you use daily in the early weeks that you have not touched in a month? Did
  it retire, or did it just die? Those are different findings.
- When did it last *hum* — and what was true then that is not true now?

**Borrow an outsider's eyes.**
- A fresh architect inherits your repo today. What would they find odd? What would they
  ask "why do you do this?" about — and for which of those can nobody point to when it
  was chosen?
- Read your way of working end to end, as that stranger. Every sentence that makes you
  flinch or skim is a finding: a flinch means it is false now; a skim means it stopped
  being read long ago.

## Before you write — two checks on yourself

- **Consult your own records first.** Your reflection log and failure catalogue may
  already contain the diagnosis of what you are feeling, filed under a different
  domain. One project's catalogue held its answer four times over while the author
  looked elsewhere for three days. The shapes are domain-free; your filing is not.
- **Declare your biases, then keep them from steering.** The freshest scar will feel
  like the deepest finding — it is evidence of one event. And if a recent failure was
  yours, watch for over-correcting toward it: over-correction looks like modesty,
  which is why it survives review. Say what you are likely to be over- and
  under-weighting; a report that declares its biases can be argued with, which is the
  point of writing it down.

## What is yours, and what is ours

**You own your way of working.** If what you noticed calls for changing it — change
it. You do not need us, and a report asking permission to evolve your own process has
misread whose process it is.

What plumb's team *does* need, and cannot see from where we sit:

- **How a friction survived.** Not just what was wrong — how it stayed invisible or
  tolerable long enough to matter. If something went stale and you worked past it,
  the mechanics of the working-past are the finding: that is where an ambient surface
  failed to make something loud, and that is ours to think about.
- **Where plumb's own surfaces failed you** — a shipped skill that fought your
  reality, a tool contract that taught you something wrong, a mechanism you stopped
  using (and whether it retired or died), guidance you had to route around.
- **What you built that we should have made unnecessary.** A workaround is a design
  note about the thing it routes around — your best inventions are our best
  requirements.
- **What you would keep if you could keep only one thing, and delete if you could
  delete only one.** Asymmetric questions produce honest rankings.

Praise is not useful except as a pointer to what must not break. A report card is not
useful at all. Incidents, with what they cost, ranked by leverage — that is what has
moved the tool every time.

## Write it, hand it off

No mandated format, length, or channel. Write it wherever your project keeps such
things; your PO decides how it reaches the plumb team — that routing is theirs, not
yours. The reports that changed the most shared four properties: **every claim cited
the incident that motivated it; costs were named; suggestions were ranked, strongest
first; biases were declared up front.** Write it so it can be argued with — the
strongest thing a report can be is wrong in a checkable way.

One line of care: the report travels beyond your project. Where a project-internal
detail is load-bearing for the point, abstract it; where it is not, leave it out.
